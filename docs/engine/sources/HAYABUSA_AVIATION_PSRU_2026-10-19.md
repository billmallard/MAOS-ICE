## 0. Bottom line

1. **No dedicated, catalogued Hayabusa PSRU product was found.** I found no vendor, product name, ratio, rating, mass or price for a Hayabusa redrive in any source I could read. The suggested candidates either use other engines (Raven Redrives: poly-V belt on automotive conversions [7][8]; Magni and ArrowCopter: Rotax 914 [29]; Ace Aviation: no Hayabusa listing [21]) or turned up nothing linking them to the Hayabusa.
2. **The one documented Hayabusa aircraft is the Bugatti 100P replica N110PX (NTSB CEN16FA307).** It ran **the motorcycle's own clutch and gearbox locked in 6th gear**, which then fed a reduction gearbox (forward engine) or a chain, shafts and U-joints (rear engine). The forward engine surged to about 10,000 rpm (redline 9,500) just after liftoff, and the airplane stalled and crashed on its 3rd flight. The NTSB's probable cause is an "engine anomaly, the reason for which could not be identified". The builders' team concluded it was **clutch slippage** [1][2][3][4][5].
3. **Closest analog with a real field record: the Yamaha Genesis 4-cylinder (RX-1/Apex YG4).** A key difference: the Yamaha has **no gearbox**, only a fixed internal countershaft (1.19:1 or 1.23:1) **with a rubber harmonic damper**. Most installs use the SkyTrax 3.83:1 gear PSRU or a Rotax C gearbox with a Hardy disc or RK400 clutch [12][13][14][15][16][17].
4. **Torsional-vibration theory (EPI Inc.):** put the system's first torsional mode **well below idle** using a soft coupling, so that the operating range sits above crossover (√2 × fn). Avoid rigid couplings and one-way (sprag) clutches [22][23][24][25].

### Bugatti 100P: NTSB CEN16FA307
- **Report number:** CEN16FA307 (preliminary PDF header: "Accident Number: CEN16FA307") [1]. AOPA cites docket EventID 20160806X23345 [4].
- **Preliminary (2016):** "The propulsion drivetrain was traced and no preimpact anomalies were detected. The engines sustained thermal damage and could not be rotated by hand. However, no indications of engine anomalies were observed." [1]
- **Factual (Nov 2017):** "The rpm indication for the left/forward engine began to climb...without any apparent TLA input from the pilot"; the forward engine reached about 10,000 rpm against a "maximum operating speed (red line) of 9,500 rpm" [3]. ASN: "The left/forward engine began surging after liftoff and reached its maximum operating speed twice during the short flight." [5]
- **Probable cause (final, Dec 2017):** "The pilot's failure to maintain airspeed following an engine anomaly during the initial climb after takeoff, which resulted in the airplane exceeding its critical angle of attack" with contributing "engine anomaly, the reason for which could not be identified during postaccident examination" [3][5].
- **Was the engine or drive implicated?** The NTSB named an engine anomaly of unknown cause; it found no pre-impact drivetrain anomaly, though the drivetrain was fire-damaged. The builders' team concluded: "Clutch slippage occurred on the forward engine, causing a loss of power at a critical stage of the flight" [4]. AvWeb: "The only reasonable conclusion is that the power failure in the forward engine drivetrain was the result of a clutch failure in the forward engine." [2] AOPA notes "past problems with the chain drive system" (rear engine) [4].
- **Design lesson:** the rpm surge with no change in thrust is the signature of a slipping **stock motorcycle wet clutch** carrying full takeoff torque through the bike transmission in top gear. That is an engineering inference, not an NTSB finding.

## 5. Torsional-vibration failures of PSRUs: published explanations

EPI Inc. (epi-eng.com engineering pages) [22][23][23b][24][25]:
- Mechanism: "If the torsional excitation from the engine is anywhere near one of the engine-gearbox-propeller resonant frequencies, the forces applied to the gears, shafts and propeller will be amplified." [22]
- "A coupling system which does not eliminate the torsional excitation has properties which increase the dynamic load on the gears dramatically" [22]
- Prop risk: "A prop subjected to torsional excitation can easily shed a blade or a portion of a blade, followed almost immediately by the whole powerplant" [22]
- Below crossover: "No matter how much damping is added, if the engine-PRSU-Propeller system is operating below crossover, the PSRU ... feels torque pulses GREATER than what the engine produces." Crossover is at r = 1.414 [23].
- Fix: design the coupling to "locate the system-first-mode resonant frequency well below engine idle speed", which gives transmissibility below 0.10. EPI's own target: "transmissibility between the engine and gearbox is less than 1%" [22][23].
- Avoid: "any PSRU that has a torsionally-rigid coupling"; "TEST STAND OPERATION IS NOT THE SAME AS FLIGHT"; "destructive vibration can (and often DOES) occur at frequencies that are far beyond the range that humans can detect" [24].
- Sprag/one-way clutch: "Suddenly, BANG, the clutch re-engages, imposing an extremely high shock load"; loads at "240 Hz to 320 Hz"; the prop freewheels when power is reduced and "can easily overspeed"; the early clutch outer race "fractured" [25]. "RUN (don't walk) away from anything with an alleged vibration-killing one-way clutch" [24].
- Idler gears are "subjected to fully-reversing loads" [23b].

Kitplanes:
- Terry Edwards (2001/2004): "A gear drive is the most reliable and the most costly"; "Toothed-belt manufacturers do not endorse PSRU applications"; chains "are prone to galling, tension failures and fatigue failures"; "Neither the crankshaft nor the crankshaft bearings was designed for side loads." [9]
- Rick Lindstrom (2010): "Two weak points of auto-engine conversions ... are the propeller speed reduction unit (PSRU) and the cooling system." Geared Drives' clutch "typically has only a few cycles per flight, so wear is minimal." [11]

Phillip Johnson (canardaviation, **personal site**): "A resonant condition is set up when operating near idle when the engine drives the propeller followed by the propeller driving the engine." [35]

Rotax 912 reference practice (search snippet from rotax-owner / VAF, **forum**): the gearbox has a dog-type torsional shock absorber with ramped dogs and springs, plus an overload clutch that slips at about 5 × running torque [36].

## 6. High-load duty evidence for the Hayabusa (airboat, hovercraft, race)

- Airboat/hovercraft: **not found** (southernairboat thread 403; no hovercraft source found).
- Best sustained-load proxy is Radical race cars, which use Hayabusa-derived 1300/1340/1500 engines:
  - Radical (manufacturer, 2024-12-03): "Radical have recommended engine refresh intervals for all their engines ranging from 40 to 80 hours." [37]
  - **forum** (CharleyH, admin): 1500 cc "no more than 40 hours" under race conditions; 1340 cc "less stressed and can go 90 to 100 hours". Reasons: "the car spends much more time at full throttle". K8 engines are refitted with K7 cam tensioners because "the newer style tensioner ... was causing cam chains to break in an automotive application" (2020-10-28) [38][39].
  - Reported wear items (**forum**): transmission gears, shift forks and selector drums; cam chain and guides; starter clutch; rod bearings [39].
